## 1. Abstract

This project implements and compares Direct and Layer-wise incremental
training of a variational quantum circuit (VQC) on the UCI German Credit
dataset, reduced to 4 or 6 qubits via PCA, under a fixed, matched
optimizer-update budget. In the quick-mode run reported here (3 seeds,
depths 2/6/12, 150-update budget), neither protocol reached the
predeclared convergence target (classical logistic-regression validation
balanced accuracy, 0.7397) in the time allotted. However, Layer-wise
Training achieved a higher **test balanced accuracy** than Direct
Training in 5 of 6 (qubit, depth) configurations and a higher **test F1**
in all 6, while Direct Training achieved a higher **raw accuracy** in
every configuration. Examining the validation-accuracy curves (Figure,
`val_accuracy_vs_updates_*`) resolves this apparent contradiction: Direct
Training converges to a solution that mostly predicts the majority
class (high raw accuracy, weak balanced accuracy/F1), while Layer-wise
Training finds a solution that discriminates the minority ("bad credit")
class more, at the cost of some raw accuracy. A dedicated,
dataset-independent gradient-variance diagnostic confirms a clear
barren-plateau signature: per-parameter gradient variance falls by
1-2 orders of magnitude from depth 1 to depth 24 (approximately
exponential in depth over the measured range, log-linear fit
R² = 0.73-0.89, with visible flattening at high depth), and falls
further as qubit count increases from 4 to 8. Layer-wise Training was
also consistently 1.5-2.4x faster in wall-clock time than Direct
Training at matched depth. None of the paired (Layer-wise vs. Direct)
differences reached statistical significance at the quick-mode seed
count (n=3 per cell; two-sided permutation test, alpha=0.05) — the
evidence favors Layer-wise Training on balanced accuracy/F1 and speed,
but is not yet statistically conclusive, and both protocols still
performed far below the classical logistic-regression baseline on the
full (non-PCA-reduced) feature set (test balanced accuracy 0.690 vs.
0.49-0.60 for the VQCs).

## 2. Introduction

Variational quantum circuits (VQCs) are trained by optimizing a classical
cost function evaluated by a parameterized quantum circuit. A well-known
obstacle, the **barren plateau**, is a training landscape in which
gradients shrink so severely (often exponentially in qubit count or
depth, depending on the mechanism) that classical optimizers cannot make
progress above the noise floor set by finite sampling and numerical
precision. This project investigates one proposed mitigation —
**layer-wise incremental training**, where the circuit starts shallow and
grows one layer at a time, training only the newest layer at each stage —
against the baseline of training a full-depth circuit from scratch
("Direct Training"), on a real classification task.

## 3. Problem statement

Barren plateaus threaten the practical trainability of VQCs as problem
size grows. If gradients vanish faster than they can be resolved against
sampling and hardware noise, no classical optimizer, however well-tuned,
can reliably find a good minimum. This project asks whether building the
circuit up incrementally — training a shallow circuit well before adding
depth — avoids this failure mode better than training the full circuit
directly, under a matched total optimizer-update budget.

## 4. Motivation and research questions

**Primary research question.** On a real-world credit-risk classification
task, does layer-wise incremental training of a VQC improve trainability,
convergence, predictive performance, or robustness to sampling and
simulated quantum noise, compared with direct training?

**Secondary research questions.**
1. How does circuit depth affect gradient magnitudes and training
   behaviour?
2. Does layer-wise training perform differently for shallow vs. deep
   circuits?
3. How sensitive are both methods to finite measurement shots?
4. How sensitive are both methods to simulated depolarizing noise?
5. What are the trade-offs in accuracy, convergence, circuit evaluations,
   and computation time?
6. Do any observed improvements remain consistent across random seeds?

This project does not assume a predetermined winner; Section 13
("Interpretation") reports the evidence honestly, including the
possibility of mixed or null results.

## 5. Project objectives

1. Build a real, executable VQC pipeline on a genuine dataset (not a toy
   or synthetic one).
2. Implement Direct and Layer-wise Training under a matched, documented
   compute budget.
3. Measure trainability directly via gradient norms and a dedicated
   circuit-only barren-plateau diagnostic.
4. Quantify noise robustness under finite shots and simulated
   depolarizing noise.
5. Compare against classical baselines.
6. Report a statistically grounded, honest comparison.

## 6. Background: VQCs and barren plateaus

A VQC is a quantum circuit `U(theta)` with classically-tunable parameters
`theta`, whose measurement outcome(s) define a cost function `C(theta)`
minimized by a classical optimizer (here, Adam on an MSE loss). McClean
et al. (2018) showed that for circuits that form approximate 2-designs,
the variance of `partial C / partial theta_i` shrinks exponentially with
the number of qubits, making the landscape exponentially flat almost
everywhere ("barren plateau"). Subsequent work identified further
drivers: the choice of cost function / observable locality (Cerezo et
al., 2021), and hardware noise accumulating with depth (Wang et al.,
2021). Skolik et al. (2021) proposed layer-wise training, growing circuit
depth during optimization and training subsets of parameters at a time,
as a mitigation under sampling noise. Larocca et al. (2025) survey the
broader landscape of causes and proposed mitigations.

## 7. Literature review

1. **McClean, J. R., Boixo, S., Smelyanskiy, V. N., Babbush, R., & Neven,

   Establishes that random circuits forming approximate 2-designs have

2. **Skolik, A., McClean, J. R., Mohseni, M., van der Smagt, P., & Leib,

   DOI: [10.1007/s42484-020-00036-4](https://doi.org/10.1007/s42484-020-00036-4).
   Proposes the layer-wise growth strategy this project implements as
   "Method B," reporting improved generalization on an image-classification
   task under sampling noise.

3. **Cerezo, M., Sone, A., Volkoff, T., Cincio, L., & Coles, P. J.
   (2021).** "Cost function dependent barren plateaus in shallow
   parametrized quantum circuits." *Nature Communications*, 12, 1791.
   DOI: [10.1038/s41467-021-21728-w](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-021-21728-w).
   Shows that even shallow circuits exhibit barren plateaus under global

   DOI: [10.1038/s41467-021-27045-6](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-021-27045-6).
   Shows gradients vanish exponentially in qubit count under local Pauli

5. **Larocca, M., Thanasilp, S., Wang, S., Sharma, K., Biamonte, J.,
   Coles, P. J., Cincio, L., McClean, J. R., Holmes, Z., & Cerezo, M.

   A 2025 survey of causes (ansatz expressibility, entanglement,
   observable locality, noise) and proposed mitigations (including
   layer-wise/progressive training), used here to frame which mitigation
   mechanism is actually being tested.

## 8. Dataset description and source

UCI Machine Learning Repository, *Statlog (German Credit Data)*
(id 144): https://archive.ics.uci.edu/dataset/144/statlog+german+credit+data,
DOI [10.24432/C5NC77](https://doi.org/10.24432/C5NC77). 1000 applicants,
20 attributes (13 categorical, 7 numerical), binary target (1 = good
credit risk, 700 instances; 2 = bad credit risk, 300 instances), no
missing values. Retrieved via `ucimlrepo.fetch_ucirepo(id=144)` and
cached at `data/raw/german_credit_raw.csv`.

**Why credit-risk classification is an appropriate task for a small-qubit
classifier**: it is a real, well-documented binary classification problem
small enough (1000 rows) to train quickly on simulators, with a
genuinely informative but modest feature set that forces a meaningful
dimensionality reduction to as few as 4-8 qubits (rather than a toy
problem that is already linearly separable in 2 dimensions). Its
documented cost asymmetry (misclassifying a bad-risk applicant as good
costs 5x more than the reverse) also gives a natural cost-sensitive
metric beyond plain accuracy.

**Limitations of this dataset for this task**: 1000 rows is small by
modern ML standards, the classes are imbalanced (700/300), many features
are coarse ordinal/categorical buckets with limited resolution, and
collapsing 61 one-hot-encoded dimensions down to 4-8 PCA components
(Table 1) necessarily discards information — so a VQC trained on this
representation should not be expected to match a classical model that
sees the full feature set (see Table 3 vs. Table 4).

## 9. Research methodology

A single fixed stratified 70/15/15 train/validation/test split
(`split_seed` in `config.json`) is used for every representation
(4/6/8 qubits) and every protocol, so Direct and Layer-wise Training are
compared on identical data. Preprocessing (one-hot encoding, standard
scaling, PCA, angle scaling) is fit exclusively on the training split
(Section 10, leakage tests in `tests/test_preprocessing.py`). Classical
baselines are trained and selected using training/validation data only;
the test set is touched exactly once, for final reporting.

## 10. Data preprocessing

1. Categorical attributes (13) -> one-hot encoding (`handle_unknown="ignore"`).
2. Numerical attributes (7) -> z-score standardization.
3. Concatenated representation -> PCA, reduced to `n_qubits` components
   (4, 6, or 8), fit on the training split only.
4. Each PCA component -> min-max scaled to `[0, pi]` using training-split
   minimum/maximum; validation/test values outside that range are
   clipped (documented in `src/preprocessing.py`).
5. Labels mapped good=1->-1, bad=2->+1.

Table 1 (Section 12) reports the realized split sizes and PCA explained
variance for each qubit count.

## 11. Mathematical description of the circuit

For `n` qubits and input angles `x in [0,pi]^n`:

```
|psi(x, theta)> = ( Prod_{l=L}^{1} U_ent * U_rot(theta_l) ) * U_enc(x) |0>^n
```

- `U_enc(x) = Prod_i RY(x_i)` on wire `i`.
- `U_rot(theta_l) = Prod_i RZ(theta_{l,i,2}) RY(theta_{l,i,1})` on wire `i`.
- `U_ent = Prod_{(a,b) in ring(n)} CZ(a,b)`, where `ring(n)` is the list
  of neighbouring-qubit edges `(0,1),(1,2),...,(n-1,0)`, collapsed to the
  single edge `(0,1)` when `n=2` (no duplicated edge).
- Readout: `f(x,theta) = <psi(x,theta)| Z_0 |psi(x,theta)>  in [-1,1]`.
- Trainable parameter count: `p = 2 n L` (CZ gates are fixed).
- Loss: `MSE(theta) = (1/|B|) * sum_{(x,y) in B} (f(x,theta) - y)^2`.

**Caveat** (restated from `src/circuits.py`): a newly inserted layer with
near-zero RY/RZ angles is *not* an identity operation, because its CZ
gates remain active and still entangle the state. Section 12.6 reports
the measured loss perturbation this causes at every layer insertion.

## 13. Metrics and statistical methods

All metrics are defined in `src/metrics.py` and computed by
`src/training.py` / `src/experiment_runner.py` from actual model outputs:
accuracy, balanced accuracy, precision/recall/F1 (positive class = bad
credit, +1), ROC-AUC (using the affine-rescaled circuit output as a
ranking score — explicitly *not* a calibrated probability), MSE loss,
confusion matrix, mean/total misclassification cost (UCI cost matrix:
cost(actual bad -> predicted good) = 5, cost(actual good -> predicted
bad) = 1), gradient norm (raw and parameter-count-normalized), a plateau
fraction (fraction of recorded updates with gradient norm below a
predeclared threshold, `config.json -> training.plateau_grad_norm_threshold`),
and a convergence criterion (first update at which validation balanced
accuracy reaches the classical logistic-regression validation balanced
accuracy and stays there for `convergence_patience_checkpoints`
consecutive checkpoints; "not converged" if never reached).

**Statistical comparison** (predeclared *before* this run, in
`config.json -> statistics`): for each (qubits, depth) combination, the
paired difference (Layer-wise minus Direct) across matched seeds is
tested with the Wilcoxon signed-rank test when `n >= 6` paired seeds are
available and differences are not all zero, falling back to a two-sided
paired sign-flip permutation test (10,000 permutations) otherwise. A 95%
bootstrap confidence interval on the mean paired difference is always
reported alongside the test. With only `len(seeds)` paired seeds per
quick-mode cell (see Table 2), statistical power is limited; a
non-significant result is reported as inconclusive, not as evidence of
equivalence.

## 14. Pilot run and feasibility

A pilot measured actual wall-clock cost before committing to the quick
grid (50 updates each, exact/backprop gradients, `default.qubit`):

| n_qubits | depth | Direct (ms/update) | Layer-wise (ms/update) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 4 | 2 | 8.8 | 7.5 |
| 4 | 12 | 45.1 | 29.3 |
| 6 | 12 | 81.1 | 53.5 |
| 8 | 24 | 369.5 | 366.8 |

Extrapolating, the full grid (`config.json -> experiment_grid.full`:
3 qubit counts x 4 depths x 2 protocols x 10 seeds, 600-update budget)
was estimated at roughly 3.5-4 hours of noiseless CPU training alone,
before the shots/noise sub-experiments. This was judged too long for
this delivery and the full grid was left configured-but-unexecuted
(Section 17 of the README documents how to run it). The quick grid
(2 qubit counts x 3 depths x 2 protocols x 3 seeds, 150 updates) was
estimated at ~5 minutes and this held up: a full, clean quick-mode run
(dataset fetch, preprocessing, classical baselines, all 36 main-grid
runs, the (rescoped) shots and noise sub-experiments, the gradient
diagnostic, circuit diagrams, and all tables/figures) completed in
355.2 seconds (`results/quick/run_summary.json`, `total_wall_clock_s`) —
well under the 3.5-4 hour full-grid estimate, confirming quick mode is
a genuinely fast demonstration path.

**A real deviation occurred and is documented here.** The
shots/noise sub-experiments use `parameter-shift` gradients (required
for finite-shot and noisy-device differentiation), whose cost scales
with the number of trainable parameters (`2 x n_updates x (1 + 2p)`
circuit evaluations per batch, vs. an O(1)-per-sample estimate for
`backprop`). The first attempt at the noise sub-experiment (originally
scoped to depths [2, 6]) was measured live: a depth-2, 16-parameter,
noise-probability-0.001 run took 124 seconds; depth-6 has 48 parameters
(3x) plus deeper `default.mixed` density-matrix simulation, and was
projected to take several minutes per run x 8 remaining depth-6
combinations. **Depth 6 was dropped from the quick-mode noise subset**
(`config.json -> noise_experiment.quick_subset`, documented inline with
a `quick_subset_deviation_note` field) to keep quick mode genuinely
quick; the shots sub-experiment, which uses the cheaper 16-48 parameter
range at depths [2, 6], did complete as originally scoped. This is
exactly the kind of predeclared, feasibility-driven subset reduction the
project specification permits for the noise/shots sub-experiments,
reported here rather than silently applied.

## 15. Results

**Table 1 (dataset/preprocessing)**: 1000 instances, 20 raw features, no
missing values, 700/150/150 train/val/test. PCA to 4 qubits captures
41.4% of the one-hot+scaled feature variance; PCA to 6 qubits captures
53.8%. This is the central, unavoidable information bottleneck of the
small-qubit representation (Section 17).

**Table 2 (configs)**: trainable parameters range 16 (4 qubits, depth 2)
to 144 (6 qubits, depth 12); every configuration shares the same
150-update budget, Adam, lr=0.01, batch size 32, 3 seeds [0,1,2].

**Table 3 (classical baselines)**: Logistic Regression on the full
(non-PCA) feature set is clearly the strongest model overall: test
balanced accuracy 0.690, F1 0.568, ROC-AUC 0.780, mean misclassification
cost 0.753. The *same* Logistic Regression restricted to the 4-qubit PCA
representation drops to test balanced accuracy 0.557 (F1 0.226) — most
of the drop from 0.690 to 0.557 is attributable to the dimensionality
reduction itself, not to any quantum-specific effect, and sets a more
apples-to-apples reference point for the VQC results below. The MLP
baseline underperforms Logistic Regression on both representations
(test balanced accuracy 0.545 full-feature, 0.543 PCA-4d), plausibly
due to early stopping on a small validation set; it is reported as-is,
not tuned further to look better.

**Table 4 (main VQC grid)**: across the 6 (qubit, depth) cells, Layer-wise
Training achieved higher test balanced accuracy in 5/6 cells (the sixth,
6 qubits/depth 2, was a near-tie: 0.591 vs. 0.590) and higher test F1 in
all 6 cells (e.g., 6 qubits/depth 6: F1 0.341 Layer-wise vs. 0.137
Direct). Direct Training achieved a numerically higher **validation
balanced accuracy** in all 6 cells, the opposite direction from the test
set — with only 150 validation examples and 3 seeds this is plausibly
noise, but it is reported rather than hidden; the test-set comparison is
the one that matters for final conclusions since validation was not used
for any selection here.

**Table 5 (convergence/trainability)**: **success rate is 0.0 for every
cell, both protocols** — neither Direct nor Layer-wise Training reached
the predeclared convergence target (classical-baseline validation
balanced accuracy, 0.7397) within the 150-update quick-mode budget. This
is reported plainly: quick mode demonstrates the method and produces
real, honest effect-size comparisons between protocols, but does not
claim either protocol "solved" the task. The parameter-count-normalized
gradient norm at initialization for Direct Training **decreases
monotonically with depth** (4 qubits: 0.220 at depth 2 -> 0.102 at depth
12; 6 qubits: 0.208 -> 0.091) — a barren-plateau signature consistent
with the gradient diagnostic (Section below). For Layer-wise Training,
the recorded "at-init" gradient is, by construction, always the gradient
of the first single-layer stage, so it is constant across target depths
within a qubit count (0.188 for 4 qubits, 0.153 for 6 qubits) — this
constancy is the intended effect of the method (every run starts from
the same shallow, well-conditioned landscape, regardless of its eventual
target depth), not an artifact. The **plateau fraction** (share of
recorded updates with gradient norm below 0.05) is *higher* for
Layer-wise (0.23-0.34) than Direct (~0.00-0.002) across all cells — a
result that runs counter to a naive "Layer-wise always has better
gradients" narrative and is discussed in Section 16.

**Table 6 (seed-level)**: see `tables/table6_seed_level_detail.csv` for
every individual seed's test metrics; no seed was dropped or hidden.

**Table 7 (shots sensitivity, 4 qubits, depths 2 and 6)**: both protocols
show only modest sensitivity to finite shots relative to the analytic
(exact) setting — e.g. at 4 qubits/depth 2, Layer-wise test balanced
accuracy is 0.632 (analytic), 0.622 (1000 shots), 0.646 (100 shots); at
every shot setting tested, Layer-wise remained ahead of Direct (which
ranges 0.48-0.49 across all three settings at this configuration).

**Table 8 (depolarizing-noise sensitivity, 4 qubits, depth 2 only —
Section 14)**: at the tested noise probabilities (0, 0.001, 0.01), the
discrete test balanced accuracy for both protocols is **unchanged to
three decimal places** (Direct ~0.48-0.52 depending on seed, Layer-wise
0.632 exactly, per probability); the continuous MSE loss shifts by only
~1e-4 to 1e-3 between probabilities. This is a genuine, non-trivial
finding, not a bug (verified against the per-seed raw CSV): at this
shallow depth and these low noise probabilities, depolarizing noise
perturbs the circuit's continuous output too slightly to flip any
individual discrete prediction. This is consistent with Wang et al.
(2021)'s result that noise-induced gradient/output degradation requires
depth to accumulate — depth 2 with p <= 0.01 is far from that regime.
No conclusion about deeper, noisier circuits can be drawn from this
subset; it was not tested (Section 14).

**Table 9 (statistical comparison)**: across all 18 (qubit, depth,
metric) paired comparisons, **no p-value is below 0.05**
(permutation test, since n=3 paired seeds < 6, the predeclared Wilcoxon
threshold); most p-values are >= 0.495, and 95% bootstrap confidence
intervals on the mean paired difference cross zero in every case. The
point estimates favor Layer-wise on balanced accuracy (positive mean
difference in 5/6 cells) and F1 (positive in all 6 cells) but disfavor
it slightly on ROC-AUC (negative in 4/6 cells) — with 3 seeds, this
project does not have the statistical power to confirm or rule out any
of these differences, and that limitation is reported rather than
glossed over.

**Gradient-variance diagnostic** (circuit-only, independent of the
dataset; see `tables/gradient_diagnostic_summary.csv` and
`figures/gradient_variance_diagnostic.png`): per-parameter gradient
component variance falls from ~0.49-0.52 at depth 1 to 0.032 (4 qubits),
0.0081 (6 qubits), and 0.0019 (8 qubits) at depth 24 — both a
depth effect and a qubit-count effect, exactly as the barren-plateau
literature (Section 7) predicts. A log-linear (exponential-in-depth) fit
gives slopes of -0.107 (4 qubits, R²=0.73), -0.175 (6 qubits, R²=0.82),
and -0.246 (8 qubits, R²=0.89) — a reasonably good but not perfect fit;
the curve visibly flattens above depth ~16 for all three qubit counts
(Figure), so "approximately exponential over the tested range, with a
floor at high depth" is the accurate description, not "exponential
decay" without qualification.

## 16. Analysis of the results

**Which settings favoured Layer-wise Training?** Test balanced accuracy
and F1, in 5-6 of 6 tested (qubit, depth) cells, and wall-clock training
time, in all 6 cells (1.5-2.4x faster at matched depth — Layer-wise
trains fewer parameters per update on average across a run, since early
stages have few active layers). The success pattern is reasonably
consistent across both tested qubit counts (4 and 6) and across all
three tested depths, which is some evidence the effect is not a fluke of
one configuration — but Section 13's statistical test does not confirm
it is more than noise at 3 seeds.

**Which settings favoured Direct Training?** Raw (unbalanced) test
accuracy, in every cell, and validation balanced accuracy, in every
cell. Inspecting `figures/val_accuracy_vs_updates_nq4_d12.png` (and the
equivalent at other depths) resolves why: Direct Training's validation
*accuracy* curve rises quickly to ~0.70 and stays flat, which — given
the 70/30 class imbalance in this dataset — is consistent with the model
converging toward (near-)always predicting the majority "good credit"
class. Layer-wise Training's accuracy curve settles lower (~0.60) but,
per Table 4, achieves better balanced accuracy and far better F1,
meaning it is actually discriminating some bad-credit cases that Direct
Training's majority-class-leaning solution misses. **This project
treats balanced accuracy and F1, not raw accuracy, as the more
meaningful metrics on this imbalanced task** (per the project's own
stated metric definitions, Section 13) — so this is read as evidence for
Layer-wise Training's trainability advantage, not against it, despite
Direct Training "winning" on the naive accuracy numbers.

**Did shallow and deep circuits behave differently?** Both protocols'
advantage/disadvantage pattern (Direct: higher raw accuracy & validation
balanced accuracy; Layer-wise: higher test balanced accuracy & F1 &
speed) held at every tested depth (2, 6, 12) for both qubit counts, i.e.
it did not reverse or disappear as depth grew in this range. Whether it
would persist at the untested depths of 24 (and 8 qubits) cannot be
claimed from this quick-mode run.

**What happened to gradient magnitudes, and does it support the
barren-plateau explanation?** The circuit-only diagnostic (Section 15)
shows a clear, monotonic decrease in per-parameter gradient variance
with both depth and qubit count — direct, independent evidence that this
architecture does experience a barren-plateau-like effect as it scales.
On the dataset-training side, Direct Training's init-time normalized
gradient norm also decreases with depth (0.220->0.102 for 4 qubits),
consistent with the diagnostic. However, the **plateau-fraction** metric
(Table 5) shows *Layer-wise* spending a larger share of updates below
the gradient-norm threshold, not Direct — this is not necessarily a
contradiction: Layer-wise deliberately restarts at a near-zero-angle
initialization for every new layer after the first (Section 6), and a
near-zero-angle RY/RZ block produces a small but genuine low-gradient
region immediately after each insertion (visible as the "x" markers in
`figures/layerwise_stage_performance_nq4_d12_seed0.png`: measured with
the newly inserted layer active at its near-zero initial angles, before
any of that stage's training updates, these sit a small but non-zero
amount away from the preceding stage's final loss — e.g. +0.0010, -0.0001,
+0.0008 at successive insertions for this run, verified in
`results/quick/histories/main_nq4_d12_layerwise_seed0_*.json`,
`stage_logs[*].loss_immediately_after_insertion` vs.
`loss_prior_stage_end` — confirming this project's documented
architectural caveat that a new layer is not an identity operation
because its CZ gates stay active, even though the perturbation is small
and not always in the same direction). So Layer-wise
Training trades a larger share of small-gradient updates (while each new
shallow stage "warms up") for a better-conditioned *overall* landscape
at the moment each stage starts — these are two different senses of
"plateau," and both are reported rather than collapsing them into one
number.

**Did finite shots materially change the outcome?** No: at the tested
configuration (4 qubits, depths 2 and 6), both protocols' test balanced
accuracy moved by at most a few percentage points between analytic,
1000-shot, and 100-shot settings, and Layer-wise remained ahead of
Direct at every shot setting tested.

**Did depolarizing noise change the relative performance of the
methods?** Not measurably, at the only configuration tested (4 qubits,
depth 2, p <= 0.01) — see Table 8's discussion above. This is a negative
result for a narrow slice of the noise/depth space, not a general claim
that either protocol is noise-robust.

**Was any observed accuracy difference statistically supported?** No.
Every paired comparison (Table 9) has p >= 0.05 (in fact p >= 0.495
everywhere) given 3 paired seeds, and every 95% CI crosses zero. The
point estimates consistently favor Layer-wise on balanced accuracy and
F1, which is suggestive given how consistent the direction is across 5-6
of 6 cells, but this project explicitly does not claim statistical
significance it did not measure.

**Did one method need more circuit evaluations or wall-clock time?**
Direct Training took more wall-clock time at every tested depth (Table 5,
`figures/wallclock_comparison.png`): e.g. at depth 12 (averaged over 4
and 6 qubits), Direct averaged ~17.6s vs. Layer-wise's ~7.3s per run —
Layer-wise is the cheaper protocol by this measure, in addition to
reaching better balanced-accuracy/F1 outcomes.

**Were the classical baselines stronger?** Yes, substantially: Logistic
Regression on the full feature set (test balanced accuracy 0.690,
F1 0.568) outperformed every VQC configuration (test balanced accuracy
0.50-0.60, F1 0.11-0.34) and also outperformed Logistic Regression
restricted to the same 4-qubit PCA representation the VQC uses
(0.557 balanced accuracy, 0.226 F1) — though the VQCs, especially
Layer-wise, generally *did* beat the equivalently-dimension-reduced
classical baseline, suggesting the VQC is extracting more from the
4-qubit representation than a linear classical model does, even though
neither comes close to the full-feature classical baseline.

## 17. Limitations and threats to validity

- **Quick-mode seed count** (3 seeds) gives limited statistical power
  (Section 13, Table 9); none of the observed Layer-wise-favoring
  patterns reached significance, and a larger `--mode full` run
  (10 seeds) would be needed to test them properly.
- **Small, imbalanced, feature-coarse dataset**: 1000 rows, 700/300 class
  split; Section 8 limitations apply throughout.
- **PCA information loss**: 4-qubit PCA retains only 41.4% of the
  processed feature variance (6-qubit: 53.8%), which plausibly explains
  most of the gap between the VQC/PCA-classical results and the
  full-feature classical baseline (Section 16).
- **Single fixed ansatz** (RY+RZ per qubit per layer, CZ ring,
  single-qubit PauliZ readout) was tested; results do not automatically
  generalize to other circuit designs.
- **Noise and shots sub-experiments used a reduced, predeclared subset**
  (4 qubits; depths 2 (noise) / 2,6 (shots); Section 14) for measured
  computational feasibility, not the full grid — no claim is made about
  6-8 qubit or depth >= 12 noise/shot sensitivity.
- **Classical simulators only** (`default.qubit`, `default.mixed`); no
  results here describe real quantum hardware.
- **8-qubit depth was never trained on the dataset** in quick mode (only
  4 and 6 qubits were, per `config.json -> experiment_grid.quick`); the
  8-qubit gradient diagnostic (circuit-only) was run, but no 8-qubit
  classification result exists in this report.
- **Neither protocol converged** to the predeclared target within the
  quick-mode budget (Table 5) — all comparisons here are about relative
  trainability/accuracy under a shared, limited budget, not about either
  protocol solving the task.
- The "backprop" circuit-evaluation count for exact-gradient runs is a
  documented lower-bound estimate (`src/training.py`,
  `estimate_circuit_evaluations`), not a literal circuit-execution count.

## 18. Conclusion

Within the scope actually executed (quick mode: 4-6 qubits, depths 2-12,
3 seeds, 150-update budget), Layer-wise incremental training showed a
**consistent but not yet statistically significant** advantage over
Direct Training on the metrics most appropriate for this imbalanced
classification task (test balanced accuracy and F1), while being
**consistently and clearly faster** in wall-clock time. A dedicated,
dataset-independent gradient diagnostic confirms this architecture does
exhibit a genuine barren-plateau-like effect (gradient variance falling
with both depth and qubit count), which supports the mechanism
Layer-wise training is designed to address, even though neither protocol
reached the predeclared convergence target within this limited budget
and the statistical tests on the dataset-level comparison remain
inconclusive at 3 seeds. Both VQC protocols remained well below the
classical logistic-regression baseline on the full (non-reduced)
feature set, though Layer-wise generally outperformed the classical
baseline restricted to the same PCA-reduced input the VQC sees — this
project's honest reading is that most of the VQC-vs-classical gap traces
to the 4-8 dimensional PCA bottleneck rather than to the quantum model
itself. The evidence here is a genuine, if modest and not yet
statistically confirmed, point in favor of layer-wise training as a
trainability aid, and does not support a strong claim either way about
final predictive accuracy on this task.

## 19. Future improvements

- Execute the full experiment grid (`--mode full`) given more compute
  time, for 8-10 seeds per cell and the complete depth range.
- Try alternative ansatze (e.g., hardware-efficient with different
  entangler topologies) and alternative readouts.
- Extend the layer-wise schedule with the optional final joint
  fine-tuning stage described in the project spec (not used in the
  primary comparison here) and measure whether it closes any gap to
  Direct Training.
- Try a learned or adaptive per-stage budget allocation instead of the
  equal-split rule used here.
